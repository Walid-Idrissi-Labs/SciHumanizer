Older adults are among the people most vulnerable to heat, and rising global temperatures have brought new attention to the consequences for their health. Thermoregulatory capacity declines with age. Chronic cardiovascular, respiratory and renal conditions are more prevalent in later life, and so is the use of medications that impair the dissipation of heat. Even moderately elevated temperatures are therefore more dangerous for older adults than for younger people. Two trends add to the urgency: the proportion of older adults is growing in many countries, and heat waves are becoming more frequent, more intense and longer as the climate changes. Understanding precisely how heat exposure leads to acute illness in this population has become a priority for public health.

Much of the epidemiological evidence on heat and health relies on temperature measured at one fixed weather station, or at a few, taken to represent an entire city or region. Heat exposure is thus treated as uniform within the city, and this assumption is increasingly recognised as a significant oversimplification. Urban areas are known to have large internal differences in temperature. Because of the urban heat island effect, differences in land cover, building density, vegetation and impervious surface produce different surface and near-surface temperatures from one neighborhood to the next, sometimes by several degrees Celsius on the same day. A resident of a dense, heavily built neighborhood with little tree canopy may experience very different, and often much more severe, heat exposure than a resident of a greener, less densely built neighborhood only a few kilometers away. With a single weather station, both are assigned the same exposure.

This gap between the usual measurement of heat exposure and the exposure that people in fact experience has implications for research and for public health policy. If a large part of the health risk is due to intra-urban variation in temperature, studies based on the city-wide average may be missing, or at least underestimating, a significant part of the true burden of urban heat. In the same way, interventions designed around city-wide heat warnings may fail to identify, or to protect adequately, the neighborhoods and residents with the greatest exposure. It is therefore necessary to establish whether, and to what extent, this fine-scale spatial variation is related to acute health outcomes, so that interventions can be targeted at the areas of greatest risk instead of being applied uniformly to the whole city.

Satellite-derived estimates of land-surface temperature (LST) now make it possible to examine this question in spatial detail that was not previously feasible. A satellite does not depend on one or a handful of ground-based weather stations. It provides LST at a resolution as fine as 1 km over an entire urban area, and so records the differences between neighborhoods that are averaged away in station-based measurements. When these data are linked to large datasets of individual health records, the analysis can go beyond the general association between extreme heat and adverse health outcomes, which is already well established in the literature: it can test whether the risk to a person depends on the part of the city in which that person is exposed.

For an analysis of this kind, emergency hospital admissions are a particularly useful outcome. They record acute, clinically significant deterioration in health more completely and more promptly than mortality data alone can. The diagnosis at admission (respiratory, renal or cardiovascular, for example) also indicates which physiological systems are most affected by heat, and this is relevant both to understanding the mechanisms and to guiding the clinical and public health response.

In this study we linked daily LST estimates at a resolution of 1 km to 1.9 million emergency hospital admissions of adults aged 65 and over in 12 European cities over the eleven years from 2008 to 2018. Using distributed-lag non-linear models, we examined whether intra-urban heat exposure, above and beyond the city-wide temperature, is associated with the risk of heat-related emergency admission, and which causes of admission are most strongly implicated.
