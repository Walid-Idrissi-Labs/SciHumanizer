Rising global temperatures have brought renewed attention to the health consequences of heat exposure, and older adults consistently emerge as one of the populations most vulnerable to these consequences. Age-related declines in thermoregulatory capacity, a higher prevalence of chronic cardiovascular, respiratory, and renal conditions, and greater likelihood of taking medications that impair the body's ability to dissipate heat all combine to make even moderately elevated temperatures more dangerous for older adults than for younger populations. As the proportion of older adults in many countries continues to grow, and as heat waves become more frequent, more intense, and longer in duration under a changing climate, understanding precisely how heat exposure translates into acute health harm in this population has become an increasingly urgent public health priority.

Much of the existing epidemiological evidence linking heat exposure to health outcomes relies on temperature measurements from a single, or a small number of, fixed weather stations representing an entire city or region. This approach implicitly treats heat exposure as a uniform citywide phenomenon, an assumption that is increasingly recognized as a significant oversimplification. Urban areas are well known to generate substantial internal temperature variation through the urban heat island effect, in which differences in land cover, building density, vegetation, and impervious surface coverage produce measurably different surface and near-surface temperatures across neighborhoods within the same city, sometimes varying by several degrees Celsius on the same day. A resident of a dense, heavily built neighborhood with little tree canopy may experience meaningfully different, and often considerably more severe, heat exposure than a resident of a leafier, less densely built neighborhood only a few kilometers away, even though both would be assigned identical temperature exposure under a single citywide weather station measurement.

This gap between how heat exposure is typically measured and how it is actually experienced across a city has important implications for public health research and policy. If health risks associated with heat are driven substantially by this intra-urban variation, then studies relying on citywide average temperature may be missing, or at least underestimating, a significant portion of the true health burden of urban heat, and public health interventions designed around citywide heat warnings may fail to identify, or adequately protect, the specific neighborhoods and residents facing the greatest actual exposure. Understanding whether, and how strongly, this fine-scale spatial variation in urban temperature relates to acute health outcomes is therefore a necessary step toward designing heat health interventions that are properly targeted rather than uniformly applied across an entire city regardless of internal variation in actual risk.

Advances in satellite-derived land-surface temperature estimation now make it possible to examine this question with a level of spatial detail that was not previously feasible. Rather than relying on one or a handful of ground-based weather stations, satellite platforms can provide land-surface temperature estimates at resolutions as fine as one kilometer across an entire urban area, capturing the kind of neighborhood-level thermal variation that citywide station-based measurements necessarily average away. Pairing this kind of high-resolution temperature data with large, individual-level health outcome datasets offers an opportunity to examine not just whether extreme heat overall is associated with adverse health outcomes, a relationship already well established in the literature, but specifically whether the location of a person's exposure within the urban heat landscape modifies their risk.

Emergency hospital admissions represent a particularly useful outcome for this kind of analysis, since they capture acute, clinically significant health deterioration with a level of completeness and timeliness that mortality data alone cannot provide, and since specific admission diagnoses, such as respiratory, renal, and cardiovascular causes, allow examination of which underlying physiological systems are most affected by heat exposure, information relevant both to understanding underlying mechanisms and to guiding clinical and public health response.

In this study, we linked high-resolution, satellite-derived land-surface temperature data to a large, multi-city dataset of emergency hospital admissions among adults aged 65 and older across twelve European cities over an eleven-year period, in order to examine whether intra-urban variation in heat exposure, above and beyond city-wide temperature, is associated with the risk of heat-related emergency hospital admission, and to identify which causes of admission are most strongly implicated in this relationship.
