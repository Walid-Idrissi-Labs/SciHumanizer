Older adults are among the people most at risk from heat, and the rise in global temperatures has brought new attention to the danger it poses to their health. The ageing body is less able to regulate its temperature. Chronic diseases of the heart, lungs and kidneys are more common in later life, and so is the use of medications that impair the loss of heat. Temperatures that are only moderately high are therefore more dangerous to older adults than to younger people. Two trends add to the urgency: the share of older adults in the population is growing in many countries, and heat waves are becoming more frequent, more intense and longer as the climate changes. Public health now needs to know precisely the way in which exposure to heat leads to acute illness in this group.

Much of the epidemiological evidence on heat and health is based on temperatures measured at one fixed weather station, or at a few, taken to represent a whole city or region. Exposure to heat is thus treated as the same in every part of the city, and this is increasingly recognised as a serious simplification. Temperatures differ widely inside a city. Because of the urban heat island effect, differences in land cover, in the density of buildings, in vegetation and in the extent of impervious surfaces produce different temperatures at and near the surface from one neighborhood to the next, sometimes by several degrees Celsius on the same day. A resident of a dense, heavily built neighborhood with few trees may be exposed to very different, and often much more severe, heat than a resident of a greener, less densely built neighborhood only a few kilometers away. With a single weather station, both are assigned the same exposure.

This gap between the way exposure to heat is usually measured and the way it is in fact experienced matters for research and for policy in public health. If a large part of the risk to health is due to differences in temperature inside the city (intra-urban differences), studies based on the average temperature of a city may be missing, or at least underestimating, a significant part of the true burden of urban heat. Interventions built on heat warnings for a whole city may also fail to identify, or to protect adequately, the neighborhoods and residents that are the most exposed. It is therefore necessary to establish whether, and to what extent, these fine-scale differences are related to acute illness, so that interventions can be targeted at the places of greatest risk instead of being applied in the same way to the whole of a city.

Estimates of land-surface temperature (LST) from satellites now make it possible to examine this question in a spatial detail that was not available before. A satellite does not depend on one or a handful of weather stations on the ground. It provides LST at a resolution as fine as 1 km over the whole of an urban area, and so records the differences between neighborhoods that are averaged away at a station. When such data are linked to large sets of health records for individual patients, the analysis can go beyond the association between extreme heat and poor health in general, which is already well established in the literature: it can test whether the risk to a person depends on the part of the city in which that person is exposed.

Emergency admissions to hospital are a particularly useful outcome for an analysis of this kind. They record acute, clinically significant deterioration in health more completely and more promptly than mortality data alone can. The diagnosis at admission (respiratory, renal or cardiovascular, for example) also shows which of the physiological systems are most affected by heat, and this is of use both in understanding the mechanisms and in guiding the clinical and public health response.

In this study we linked daily estimates of LST at a resolution of 1 km to 1.9 million emergency hospital admissions of adults aged 65 and over in 12 European cities over the eleven years from 2008 to 2018. With distributed-lag non-linear models we examined whether exposure to heat inside the city, above and beyond the temperature of the city as a whole, is associated with the risk of emergency admission related to heat, and which causes of admission are most strongly implicated.
