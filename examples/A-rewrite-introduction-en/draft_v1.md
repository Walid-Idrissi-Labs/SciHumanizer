Older adults are among the people most at risk from heat, and the rise in global temperatures has brought new attention to the danger that heat poses to their health. As the body ages it becomes less able to regulate its own temperature. Chronic diseases of the heart, the lungs and the kidneys are also more common in later life, and older adults are more likely to be taking medications that impair the loss of heat from the body. Because of these changes, temperatures that are only moderately high are more dangerous to older adults than to younger people. The problem is becoming more urgent for two reasons: the share of older adults in the population of many countries is growing, and heat waves are becoming more frequent, more intense and longer as the climate changes. There is therefore a pressing need in public health to establish the way in which exposure to heat leads to acute illness in this group.

Much of the epidemiological evidence on heat and health is based on temperatures measured at one fixed weather station, or at a small number of them, which are taken to represent an entire city or region. In such studies the exposure to heat is treated as if it were the same in every part of the city. This is a simplification, and it is increasingly recognised to be a serious one. It is well known that there are large differences in temperature inside a city. They are produced by the urban heat island effect, in which differences in land cover, in the density of buildings, in vegetation and in the extent of impervious surfaces lead to different temperatures at and near the surface in different neighborhoods of the same city; on the same day the difference can be as much as several degrees Celsius. A resident of a dense, heavily built neighborhood with few trees may be exposed to heat that is very different from, and often a good deal more severe than, the heat to which a resident of a greener and less densely built neighborhood only a few kilometers away is exposed. Yet in a study based on a single weather station the two would be given exactly the same exposure.

This gap between the way in which exposure to heat is usually measured and the way in which it is in fact experienced has important implications for research and for policy in public health. If a large part of the risk to health is due to the differences in temperature inside a city (intra-urban differences), then studies based on the average temperature of the city may be missing, or at least underestimating, a significant part of the true burden of urban heat on health. In the same way, interventions that are built on heat warnings for a whole city may fail to identify, or to protect adequately, the neighborhoods and the residents that are in fact the most exposed. It is therefore necessary to establish whether these fine-scale differences in temperature are related to acute illness, and to what extent, before interventions can be targeted at the places of greatest risk instead of being applied in the same way to the whole of a city.

Advances in the estimation of land-surface temperature (LST) from satellites now make it possible to examine this question in a degree of spatial detail that was not available before. A satellite does not depend on one or a handful of weather stations on the ground: it can provide estimates of LST at a resolution as fine as 1 km for the whole of an urban area, and so it records the differences between neighborhoods that are averaged away when measurements are taken at a station. When temperature data of this resolution are linked to large sets of health records for individual patients, it becomes possible to examine more than the association between extreme heat and poor health in general, which is already well established in the literature. It can also be asked whether the risk to a person depends on the part of the city in which that person is exposed.

Emergency admissions to hospital are a particularly useful outcome for an analysis of this kind, for two reasons. The first is that they record acute and clinically significant deterioration in health, and they do so more completely and more promptly than mortality data alone can. The second is that the diagnosis at admission (for example a respiratory, renal or cardiovascular cause) shows which of the physiological systems are most affected by heat. This information is of use both in understanding the mechanisms involved and in guiding the clinical and public health response.

In this study we linked daily estimates of LST at a resolution of 1 km to 1.9 million emergency hospital admissions of adults aged 65 and over in 12 European cities during the 11 years from 2008 to 2018. With distributed-lag non-linear models we examined whether exposure to heat inside the city, above and beyond the temperature of the city as a whole, is associated with the risk of an emergency admission related to heat, and we identified the causes of admission that are most strongly implicated in this association.
