Most public libraries in this country close by 6 or 8 p.m., some earlier, and treat that schedule as simply how libraries work, the way a bank or a post office keeps business hours. I want to make the case that this assumption deserves to be questioned, and that libraries that extend their hours until midnight would serve their communities in ways that a standard daytime schedule simply cannot.

Start with who actually gets shut out by early closing times. A library that closes at 6 p.m. is closed for the entire block of time between when most working adults leave their jobs and when they go to bed. It's closed during the hours a shift worker, someone working retail, food service, or healthcare on an evening schedule, has off. It's closed during the hours a parent finally has quiet time after getting kids to bed, or the hours a student living in a crowded apartment might actually be able to concentrate on homework. The people who most need free, quiet, well-lit public space, precisely because they don't have much of it at home or don't have the schedule flexibility to use a library during a 9-to-5 window, are disproportionately the people a standard library schedule excludes.

There's also a practical case rooted in what libraries have already become. Modern public libraries are not simply rooms of books; they are, in many communities, one of the only remaining free indoor public spaces that doesn't require a purchase to occupy. They provide internet access to people who don't have reliable broadband at home, printers and quiet space to people working on job applications or school assignments, and simple physical shelter and warmth to people who have nowhere else to be during difficult hours. All of these needs exist just as much at 9 p.m. as they do at 2 p.m., and in some respects, more; a person without stable housing needs an indoor public space more urgently in the evening, as temperatures drop and other options close, than in the middle of a weekday afternoon.

I recognize the obvious objection: cost. Extending library hours to midnight means paying staff for additional shifts, additional security, additional utilities, all at a time when many municipal budgets are already strained and libraries are frequently among the first departments to see funding cuts. This is a real constraint, not an excuse to dismiss. But it's worth noting that a handful of library systems that have experimented with extended evening hours, often just a few nights a week rather than every night, have reported strong enough usage to justify the additional cost, particularly among students and shift workers who had previously been effectively unable to use the library at all. A modest, phased expansion, extended hours on a few nights per week rather than a full seven-night-a-week midnight closing, could test this model without requiring the full cost commitment up front.

There's also a case to be made about staffing that doesn't require paying every librarian to work an overnight-adjacent shift. A smaller crew focused on quiet study space, security, and basic circulation, rather than a fully staffed reference desk, could keep a reading room and computer access open later at a fraction of full daytime staffing costs, an arrangement some university libraries already use successfully for their own extended overnight study hours during exam periods.

None of this requires every library in the country to adopt identical extended hours immediately. It requires city governments and library boards to stop treating a daytime-only schedule as an unquestioned default and start asking a more specific question: who in our community actually needs this space, and when do they need it? For a meaningful number of people, shift workers, students, parents, people without stable housing, the honest answer is going to include hours well past when the doors currently close. A library that only serves the people free during a 9-to-5 window isn't failing anyone through malice. It's just operating on an assumption about who needs it that stopped being accurate a long time ago.
