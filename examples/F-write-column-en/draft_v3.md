Many public libraries close at eight or nine in the evening, which is about when a lot of people finally have time to use them. Libraries should stay open until midnight: not every branch, and not every night, but enough of them that the late shift of a city's life has somewhere to go.

Think about who is free after nine: a nurse coming off a late shift, a cook between the dinner rush and closing time, a teenager who shares a bedroom with two siblings and needs a table and some quiet to finish an essay, someone who has just moved to the city and has no internet at home yet. For all of them the library is the obvious place. It is free, warm in winter and cool in summer, and it also has wifi, computers, printers and chairs. At nine o'clock it closes.

There is not much else to choose from at that hour. Cafés close or get loud; fast-food restaurants tolerate you for the price of a coffee; bars are bars. A library is the rare public building in which nothing is asked of the people inside except that they keep their voices down. That is the whole point of a public library, and it makes little sense to provide it only during the hours when most of the people who need it are at work.

There are three fair objections.

The first is money. Three more hours a night means more staff time, and library budgets are tight. But the late hours do not have to be the full service. Nobody needs a children's story hour at eleven at night, or a fully staffed reference desk. What is needed is the reading room, the wifi and the printers, with one or two members of staff who are trained to run a public space, and such a shift needs fewer people than a busy Saturday afternoon. A city also does not have to open every branch late. It could pick two or three branches near transit and near the neighborhoods where shift workers and students live, run them as a pilot for a year (long enough to see the winter and the exam seasons), and count who comes, hour by hour, and what they use.

The second is safety, and the third is that nobody will come. People picture a half-empty building at midnight and worry about who might wander in. Late opening would need trained staff and a clear conduct policy, the same things on which a busy daytime library already relies, and a lit, staffed building with people working quietly inside is more likely to make a street feel safer than an empty, dark one. As for demand, that question can be answered by the pilot. A count of visitors after nine costs very little, and it would settle an argument that is otherwise made on guesswork. If the late hours are still empty after six months, close them and spend the money elsewhere. My guess is that they will fill slowly, and with exactly the people who are left out by the daytime schedule.

Libraries already lend laptops, host tax clinics and help people apply for jobs; they have changed what they do many times. Changing when they do it would be one of the smaller changes. The building, the books, the wifi and the staff who know how to run a public space are all there already. What is missing is a few hours at the end of the day, and for a lot of people those are the only hours they have.
