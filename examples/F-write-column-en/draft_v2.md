Many public libraries close at eight or nine in the evening, which is about the time when a lot of people are at last free to use them. They should stay open until midnight. Not every branch, and not every night; but there should be enough of them that the people who work or study late have somewhere to go.

Think about who is free after nine. There is the nurse coming off a late shift, and the cook between the dinner rush and closing time. There is the teenager who shares a bedroom with two siblings and needs a table and some quiet to finish an essay, and the newcomer to the city who has no internet at home yet. For all of them the library is the obvious place. It is free, it is warm in winter and cool in summer, and it has wifi, computers, printers and chairs. At nine o'clock it is closed.

There is not much else on offer at that hour. Cafés are closed or loud. A fast-food restaurant will put up with you for the price of a coffee, and a bar is a bar. The library is one of the few public buildings in which nothing is asked of the people inside, except that they keep their voices down. That is what a public library is for, and it makes little sense to provide it only in the hours when most of the people who need it are at work.

Three objections are usually made, and all three are fair.

The first is money. Three more hours a night means more staff time, and library budgets are tight. But a late opening does not have to be the full service. Nobody needs a children's story hour at eleven at night, or a reference desk with three librarians; what is needed is the reading room, the wifi, the printers, and one or two members of staff who are trained to run a public space. A quiet building at night also needs fewer people than the same building on a busy Saturday afternoon. Nor does a city have to open every branch late. It could choose two or three branches that are close to public transport and to the neighborhoods where shift workers and students live, run them late for a year as a trial (a year is long enough to include the winter and the exam seasons), and count the people who come.

The second objection is safety. People picture a half-empty building at midnight and worry about who might wander in. Late opening would certainly need trained staff and a clear code of conduct, but these are the same things on which a busy library already depends during the day. A building that is lit and staffed, with people working quietly inside, is also more likely to make a street feel safe than one that is dark and empty.

The third objection is that nobody will come, and this is the one that a trial can settle. If the late hours are still empty after six months, they should be dropped and the money spent on something else. My guess is that they will fill slowly, and that they will fill with exactly the people who are left out by the daytime timetable.

Libraries have changed what they do many times: they lend laptops now, they host tax clinics, and they help people to apply for jobs. To change the hours at which they do it would be one of the smaller changes. The building, the books, the wifi and the staff are all there already. What is missing is a few hours at the end of the day, and for a lot of people those are the only hours that they have.
