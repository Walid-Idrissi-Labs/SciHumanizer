The network recorded 212,000 bat passes over 14 months with 38 sensors costing US$45 each, or US$1,710 for the acoustic hardware of the entire network. Along 60 km of highway this corresponds to an average spacing of about 1.6 km between sensors. We regard this as the principal result of the study: continuous acoustic monitoring of a road at this density, and for more than a year, is feasible at this cost. The data also contain a strong ecological signal, and they have limitations that must be considered when that signal is interpreted.

Bat activity within 50 m of lit road sections was 61% lower than at unlit sections. A reduction of this magnitude is consistent with the avoidance of artificial light described for many bat species. However, our design cannot separate the effect of lighting from that of the conditions that usually accompany it. Lit sections are often located near junctions and settlements, where traffic is heavier and the roadside habitat differs, and each of these factors could reduce activity independently of light. Until a more direct test is available, the 61% should be regarded as an upper limit for the effect of lighting alone. Such a test would compare the same sections with the lights on and off, for example where the road authority dims or switches off the lighting late at night. There is also a spatial limitation: because the comparison was restricted to the first 50 m from the road, the distance at which activity recovers is unknown.

The temporal coverage is limited in a similar way. The recordings span 14 months and therefore a single annual cycle. Seasonal differences in activity can be described, but we cannot determine whether they recur from one year to the next. A second year of recording at the same positions would show whether the effect of lighting is stable or varies with season and weather.

Because 212,000 passes cannot be checked manually, the value of such a network depends on automated identification. In a validation sample of 2,000 calls, the classifier agreed with expert manual review in 89% of cases, which leaves about 220 calls classified differently. This level of agreement is probably adequate for indices of total activity and for most questions concerning the bat community as a whole. It is not adequate for the two Myotis species, which the classifier confused in 23% of cases. For these species, results should be pooled at genus level or the calls verified manually. If the two species respond differently to lighting, the confusion could also conceal that difference, and the lighting effect should not be attributed to either species separately. The most direct remedy is to add manually verified Myotis calls from our own recordings to the training data of the classifier; some are already available in the validation sample.

Nine of the 38 sensors (24%) failed in winter because of battery problems. In temperate regions, bat activity is lowest in winter. If this also holds at our sites, the failures have little influence on the annual totals, but they preclude any conclusion about winter activity, which was recorded by only three quarters of the network. Batteries rated for low temperatures, or better insulated enclosures, would probably solve the problem. Either would increase the unit cost, by an amount that we have not yet determined.

The strength of the network is therefore its coverage, not the precision of the individual sensor. At US$45 per sensor, a road authority could equip every lit section of a road corridor and follow, month by month, the response of bat activity to a change in lighting. The two weaknesses identified here (the confusion between the Myotis species and the winter failures) are specific, and both can be corrected before such a network is deployed on a larger scale.
