The network recorded 212,000 bat passes in 14 months with 38 sensors that cost US$45 each, that is, US$1,710 for the acoustic hardware of the whole network. The sensors were spread over 60 km of highway, which gives an average spacing of about 1.6 km. We consider this to be the main result of the study: at this price, a road can be monitored acoustically at this density and for more than a year. The data also contain a strong ecological signal, and they have limitations that must be taken into account when the signal is interpreted.

Bat activity within 50 m of lit sections of the road was 61% lower than at unlit sections. A reduction of this size is consistent with the avoidance of artificial light that has been described for many species of bats. The design of the study, however, does not allow the effect of light to be separated from the effects of the conditions that are usually associated with it. Lit sections are often located near junctions and settlements, where there is more traffic and where the habitat at the roadside is different, and each of these factors could reduce activity independently of light. The 61% should therefore be regarded as an upper limit of the effect of lighting alone. The effect could be tested more directly by comparing the same sections with the lights on and off, for example in places where the lighting is dimmed or switched off late at night by the road authority. There is also a spatial limitation: the comparison was restricted to the first 50 m from the road, and the distance at which activity recovers is therefore not known.

A similar limitation applies to time. The recordings cover 14 months, and so a single annual cycle. Seasonal differences in activity can be described, but it cannot be established that they are repeated from one year to the next. A second year of recording at the same positions would show whether the effect of lighting is stable or varies with the season and the weather.

The usefulness of a network of this kind depends on automatic identification, because 212,000 passes cannot be checked by hand. In a validation sample of 2,000 calls, the classifier agreed with manual review by an expert in 89% of cases, so that about 220 calls were assigned differently. This level of agreement is probably sufficient for indices of total activity and for most questions that concern the bat community as a whole. It is not sufficient for the two Myotis species, which were confused by the classifier in 23% of cases. The results for these two species should be pooled at the level of the genus, or the calls should be verified manually. Because of the confusion, a difference between the two species in their response to lighting could also remain undetected, and the effect of lighting should not be attributed to either species separately. The most direct remedy is to add manually verified Myotis calls from our own recordings to the training data of the classifier; some of these calls are already available in the validation sample.

Nine of the 38 sensors (24%) failed in winter because of battery problems. In temperate regions winter is the season in which the activity of bats is lowest. If this is also the case at our sites, the failures have little effect on the annual totals, but they prevent any conclusion about activity in winter, which was recorded by only three quarters of the network. Batteries rated for low temperatures or better insulated enclosures would probably solve the problem. Both would increase the cost of a unit, by an amount that we have not yet determined.

The strength of the network is therefore its coverage, and not the precision of the individual sensor. At US$45 per sensor, every lit section of a road corridor could be equipped by a road authority, and the response of bat activity to a change in lighting could be followed month by month. The two weaknesses that we identified, the confusion between the Myotis species and the failures in winter, are specific, and both can be corrected before a network of this kind is deployed on a larger scale.
