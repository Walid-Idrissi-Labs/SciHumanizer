At US$45 each, the 38 sensors we deployed along 60 km of highway recorded 212,000 bat passes in 14 months. We think this is the most useful result of the study: acoustic monitoring at the scale of a road network is now cheap enough to deploy densely and leave in place for more than a year. The data also carry a strong ecological signal, and they have limits that matter for how that signal should be read.

Bat activity within 50 m of lit road sections was 61% lower than along unlit sections. A drop of that size is consistent with the light avoidance described for many bat species, but our design cannot separate light from what usually comes with it. Lit sections are often the ones near junctions and settlements, where traffic is heavier and roadside habitat differs, and any of these could reduce activity on its own. A sharper test would compare the same sections with the lights on and off, for example where a road authority dims or switches off lighting late at night. Until then, we read the 61% as an upper bound on the effect of light alone. The comparison also has a spatial limit. It covers the first 50 m from the road, so it says nothing about how far from a lit section bat activity recovers.

The 14-month record has a similar limit in time. It spans one full annual cycle, so seasonal differences in activity along the highway can be described, but not whether they repeat from one year to the next. A second year of recording at the same sensor positions would show whether the light effect is stable or varies with season and weather.

Nobody can review 212,000 passes by hand, so the value of a network like this rests on the classifier. The classifier agreed with expert manual review for 89% of a 2,000-call validation sample. That is probably good enough for activity indices and for most questions about the bat community as a whole. It is not good enough for the two Myotis species, which the classifier confused in 23% of cases. Results for those two species should be pooled at genus level or checked by hand; the confusion could also mask real differences in how the two respond to lighting, so the light effect should not be read as applying to either species separately. Adding manually verified Myotis calls from our own recordings to the training data is the most direct fix, and the validation sample already contains some of them.

Nine sensors failed during winter because of battery problems, nearly a quarter of the network. If winter is the season of lowest bat activity at our sites, as it is in temperate regions, the loss matters less for annual totals than for any question about winter activity itself, which our data cannot answer. Cold-rated batteries or better-insulated enclosures are the obvious fixes. Both would raise the unit cost, and we do not yet know by how much.

What the network offers is breadth rather than precision. At US$45 per sensor, a road authority could instrument every lit section of a corridor and follow, month by month, whether changes to lighting change bat activity. The two weaknesses we found (Myotis confusion and winter failure) are specific enough to fix before a network like this is scaled up.
