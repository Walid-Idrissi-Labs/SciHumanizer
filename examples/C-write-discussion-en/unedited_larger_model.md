The scale of acoustic activity we recorded, over 212,000 bat passes across 60 kilometers of highway over fourteen months, demonstrates that a distributed network of low-cost acoustic sensors can generate a dataset of a size and spatial resolution that would be essentially impossible to obtain through traditional manual survey methods at anything close to comparable cost. At roughly forty-five dollars per unit, the sensors used in this study cost a small fraction of commercially available bioacoustic monitoring equipment, and our results suggest that this cost reduction did not come at a prohibitive cost to data quality, at least for the primary purpose of detecting and broadly characterizing bat activity along a road corridor.

The most striking finding to emerge from this dataset is the pronounced reduction in bat activity, 61 percent, within fifty meters of lit sections of highway compared to unlit sections. This magnitude of effect is considerably larger than what several prior, smaller-scale studies of artificial light and bat activity have reported, and it likely reflects some combination of direct light avoidance behavior in light-averse bat species and the specific characteristics of highway lighting, which tends to be continuous, high-intensity, and fixed in position along the entire lit segment, rather than the more localized or intermittent lighting sources examined in some previous studies. Because our sensor network sampled continuously over more than a year, this finding also captures activity across multiple seasons and weather conditions, lending it more confidence than a result derived from a shorter, single-season sampling effort. The practical implication is fairly direct: stretches of highway lighting appear to impose a substantial cost on bat activity in immediately adjacent habitat, a cost that could plausibly be reduced through changes to lighting design, timing, or placement along sensitive corridor segments.

The performance of our automated species classifier deserves a more qualified interpretation. An 89 percent agreement rate with expert manual review across our full validation sample indicates that the classifier performed well overall and captures enough accuracy to support the kind of broad activity and habitat-use patterns that motivated this study. However, the specific difficulty distinguishing between two Myotis species, confused in 23 percent of relevant validation calls, is a meaningful limitation rather than a minor footnote. Myotis species are notoriously difficult to distinguish acoustically even for experienced human analysts, given the overlapping call structure and frequency range many species in this genus share, so this particular source of classifier error is not surprising, but it does mean that any conclusions drawn from this dataset about the relative activity or distribution of these two specific species individually should be treated with considerably more caution than conclusions about overall bat activity or activity of more acoustically distinct species.

The loss of nine sensors to battery failure during winter months represents a practical, logistical limitation of the network rather than a data quality concern in the strict sense, but it does mean that our winter activity estimates are based on a smaller effective sample of monitoring locations than our estimates for other seasons, and it points to a specific area for improvement in future deployments of this kind of low-cost sensor network. Cold temperatures are a well-known stressor for the battery chemistries commonly used in low-cost field electronics, and future iterations of this monitoring approach would likely benefit from cold-rated battery options, improved insulation, or solar-assisted charging, any of which would add modestly to per-unit cost but could meaningfully improve year-round data completeness, an important consideration given that highway lighting and traffic effects on bat activity may vary seasonally in ways that a network with reduced winter coverage cannot fully characterize.

Taken together, these results support the feasibility of low-cost acoustic sensor networks as a practical tool for highway-scale bat activity monitoring, while also indicating specific areas, species-level classification accuracy for acoustically similar taxa and winter equipment reliability, where the current approach has clear room for improvement. For agencies and researchers considering similar deployments, our results suggest that this approach can support robust conclusions about broad patterns of activity and light avoidance at meaningful spatial and temporal scales, while species-specific conclusions, particularly for acoustically similar species pairs, should be corroborated with additional validation before being used to inform species-specific management or mitigation decisions.
