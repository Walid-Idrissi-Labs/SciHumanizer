The network recorded 212,000 bat passes in 14 months with 38 sensors that cost US$45 each, that is, US$1,710 for the acoustic hardware of the whole network. Spread over 60 km of highway, the sensors were about 1.6 km apart on average. We consider this to be the main result of the study: at this price, a road can be monitored acoustically at this density and for more than a year. The data also contain a strong ecological signal, and they have limitations that must be kept in mind when that signal is interpreted.

Within 50 m of lit sections of the road, bat activity was 61% lower than at unlit sections. A reduction of this size is consistent with the avoidance of artificial light that has been described for many species of bats. Our design, however, cannot separate the effect of light from the effects of the conditions that usually go with it. Lit sections are often near junctions and settlements, where there is more traffic and the roadside habitat is different, and each of these factors could reduce activity independently of light. Until a more direct test is made, the 61% should be regarded as an upper limit of the effect of lighting alone. Such a test would compare the same sections with the lights on and off, for example where the road authority dims the lighting or switches it off late at night. There is also a spatial limitation: because we compared only the first 50 m from the road, we do not know at what distance activity recovers.

A similar limitation applies to time. The recordings cover 14 months, and so a single annual cycle. Seasonal differences in activity can be described, but we cannot tell whether they are repeated from one year to the next. With a second year of recording at the same positions, it would be possible to show whether the effect of lighting is stable or varies with the season and the weather.

Because 212,000 passes cannot be checked by hand, the usefulness of a network of this kind depends on automatic identification. In a validation sample of 2,000 calls, the classifier agreed with manual review by an expert in 89% of cases, so that about 220 calls were assigned differently. This level of agreement is probably sufficient for indices of total activity and for most questions that concern the bat community as a whole. It is not sufficient for the two Myotis species, which the classifier confused in 23% of cases. For these two species, the results should be pooled at the level of the genus, or the calls should be verified manually. If the two species respond differently to lighting, the confusion could also hide the difference, and the effect of lighting should not be attributed to either species separately. The most direct remedy is to add manually verified Myotis calls from our own recordings to the training data of the classifier; some of these calls are already available in the validation sample.

Nine of the 38 sensors (24%) failed in winter because of battery problems. In temperate regions winter is the season in which the activity of bats is lowest. If this is also the case at our sites, the failures have little effect on the annual totals, but they prevent any conclusion about activity in winter, which was recorded by only three quarters of the network. Batteries rated for low temperatures or better insulated enclosures would probably solve the problem. Both would increase the cost of a unit, by an amount that we have not yet determined.

The strength of the network is therefore its coverage, not the precision of the individual sensor. At US$45 per sensor, a road authority could equip every lit section of a road corridor and follow, month by month, the response of bat activity to a change in lighting. The two weaknesses that we identified (the confusion between the Myotis species and the failures in winter) are specific, and both can be corrected before a network of this kind is deployed on a larger scale.
