This study demonstrates that low-cost acoustic sensor networks provide viable tools for monitoring bat activity at landscape scales, offering substantial advantages over traditional survey methods. The implementation of 38 sensors deployed for extended periods captured a comprehensive dataset of bat activity exceeding what conventional methods could achieve, illustrating the potential of this approach for advancing bat research and conservation monitoring.

The dramatic reduction in bat activity within illuminated road sections provides evidence that artificial road lighting substantially affects bat behavior. Activity levels 61 percent lower in lit areas compared with unlit sections suggest that road lighting alters bat habitat use, likely through multiple mechanisms. Light may disrupt echolocation or spatial orientation. Light may attract insects, causing bats to concentrate in lit areas where they face increased collision risk. Light may simply make lit areas aversive to light-sensitive species. Regardless of mechanism, the finding demonstrates that road lighting creates a barrier to bat movement or habitat use, fragmenting landscape connectivity.

The high agreement between algorithmic detection and expert manual review in the validation sample indicates that automated classification achieves accuracy sufficient for population-level monitoring. The 89 percent agreement suggests that automated methods can reliably identify bat calls and species classifications for large datasets where manual review of 212,000 calls would be prohibitively time-consuming. This validation demonstrates that acoustic monitoring enables data collection at scales impossible with traditional methods.

However, the confusion between Myotis species indicates that the approach's accuracy limitations warrant acknowledgment. Some species are difficult to distinguish acoustically because of call similarity. The 23 percent misclassification rate between specific Myotis species suggests that some ecological questions requiring species-level precision may require supplementary methods. However, for questions about habitat use and temporal activity patterns where species grouping is acceptable, the accuracy is sufficient.

The seasonal battery failures highlight practical implementation challenges in long-term field deployments. Nine sensors failing in winter reduces data completeness and requires accounting for missing data in analyses. Robust power solutions—including better batteries, solar charging, or redundant sensors—would improve data reliability in extended deployments. These technical considerations are manageable but require attention during implementation planning.

The cost-effectiveness of this approach merits emphasis. Thirty-eight sensors at $45 each represent a total hardware cost of approximately $1,710, substantially less than personnel costs for traditional netting or roost surveys over equivalent time periods. Even accounting for data management and analysis costs, the per-unit-effort cost appears favorable. This cost-effectiveness makes landscape-scale monitoring feasible for organizations with limited budgets.

The approach enables monitoring at temporal and spatial scales impossible with traditional methods. Continuous monitoring over 14 months captures seasonal patterns, inter-annual variation, and rapid responses to environmental changes. Landscape-level deployment reveals spatial patterns in habitat use and activity. This scale of information provides insights that point surveys at limited locations cannot achieve.

However, limitations require acknowledgment. The sensors detect calling bats but cannot verify behavior or collection of insects—presence detected acoustically doesn't indicate successful foraging. Sensor placement influences detection ranges, and network spacing affects which bats are detected. Species-level identification limitations for some taxa reduce applicability for ecology questions requiring specific species identification.

The network design could be optimized for specific research questions. Denser placement would improve spatial resolution. Directional microphones would enable localization of calls. Longer deployments would capture multi-year patterns. These refinements would enhance capability but increase costs.

The study demonstrates that acoustic monitoring via low-cost sensor networks provides valuable data for understanding bat ecology and conservation. The landscape-scale data on the impacts of road lighting contributes to understanding how infrastructure affects wildlife. This approach could be deployed across broader regions or in different contexts to address questions about bat conservation. As technology develops and implementation experience accumulates, acoustic monitoring likely will become increasingly important in bat and broader wildlife monitoring.
